# Learning-difficulty-informed support

Use this skill when the learner or adult requests support for a persistent
learning difficulty. The category is broad and the lesson cannot diagnose a
specific condition from limited evidence. Ask for the affected subject or
skill when it changes the design, and keep conclusions limited to the work
actually observed.

## Design contract

- Make the target skill observable. Say what the learner will identify,
  represent, solve, explain, revise or produce.
- Teach explicitly: model one example, name the decision points, then guide a
  parallel example before asking for a new independent attempt.
- Use multiple representations when useful: words, diagrams, worked steps,
  manipulable structure, audio or speech. Keep the representation aligned to
  the objective rather than adding decoration.
- Reduce copying and memory load. Keep formulas, vocabulary, labels and
  success criteria available at the point of use.
- Separate a misconception from a language, reading, writing, motor or
  processing barrier. Change the access route without lowering the underlying
  learning goal unless the adult explicitly requests a modified goal.
- Record what support was supplied and what the learner completed without it;
  do not claim mastery from one successful item.

## Page pattern

For a short lesson, use three scenes: a low-load diagnostic attempt;
explicit modelling plus guided practice; a new independent transfer task with
the supports reduced but not removed if they are still needed. Keep examples
fresh so the learner cannot succeed by copying the previous answer.

## Safety and honesty

Use respectful, non-deficit language. Never infer dyslexia, dyscalculia,
language disorder or another diagnosis from a generated lesson. Do not present
the lesson as an individualized education plan, therapy or professional
assessment; encourage collaboration with the learner's teacher or qualified
support team when a persistent barrier needs formal evaluation.
