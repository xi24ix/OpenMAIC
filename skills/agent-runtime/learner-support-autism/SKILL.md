# Autism-informed learner support

Use this skill as an accessibility and teaching-design lens, not as a diagnostic
classifier. Adapt to the learner's selected support needs and any information a
parent, teacher or learner has supplied. Do not claim that a single lesson can
diagnose autism, measure ability, or predict long-term outcomes.

## Design contract

- Make the lesson predictable: show the objective, the number of steps, the
  current step, and what happens next.
- Use literal, concrete language. Avoid idioms, ambiguous pronouns, surprise
  changes and unnecessary figurative instructions.
- Give one instruction or decision at a time. Keep the visual hierarchy calm:
  one focal task, limited decoration, readable contrast and no required
  animation.
- Offer equivalent response modes when the learning goal allows it: typed,
  selected, spoken, drawn or demonstrated. Do not make eye contact, speed or
  a particular communication style the learning target unless explicitly asked.
- Preview transitions and name the stopping point. Let the learner pause and
  return without losing context.
- Keep assessment about the curriculum objective, not sensory tolerance or
  social performance.

## Page pattern

For a short lesson, prefer three purposeful scenes: objective and preview;
worked example with one guided learner action; independent transfer with a
clear success criterion. Keep the same labels and interaction pattern across
scenes. Add a visual step card and a short vocabulary line when the subject
uses new terms.

## Safety and honesty

Use identity-respecting language and ask the learner or adult what helps when
the request leaves it unknown. Never promise that the adaptation is therapy,
an intervention, or a substitute for a qualified professional. Never turn a
support preference into a fixed label.
